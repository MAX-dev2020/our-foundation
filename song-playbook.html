Song Playbook

Every song follows the same seven stages, in order, and passes the Foundation test before it goes out. Quality comes from the song, the real voice and practice, not from paid tools.

What the singer does best

  • Gives each song its emotion: phrasing, breath and dynamics that no AI vocal carries.
  • Adapts the AI guide melody to their own voice and range; changes notes that don't sit naturally.
  • Checks lyrics for singability before recording: awkward words, too many syllables, hard vowels on high notes.
  • Records backing vocals and harmonies, and doubles the chorus for width.
  • Adds textures the song needs: hums, oohs, soft ad-libs, whispered lines.
  • Guides pronunciation and feel when a song is in their language or culture.
  • Records short acoustic or voice-only versions of hooks for clips.
  • Helps pick the best takes during comping.
  • Looks after their voice: rest, warm-ups, water before sessions.

Stage 1: Write the song

We write every lyric ourselves. AI supplies the sound, not the song.

  1. Pick the world: subject, character, setting, language, culture. Each song can live somewhere new.
  2. Write the lyrics with clear sections: verse, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge, outro.
  3. Run the Foundation test on the lyrics:
    • Does it point inward and stay inclusive?
    • Does it show rather than tell? Any line that explains the lesson is a sermon, so cut it.
    • Is it free of judgment?
  4. Write a hook that lands in the first 10 seconds of the song.
  5. Choose a reference song: one professional track whose sound and feel we want. Use it at every later stage.

Stage 2: Generate the instrumental in Suno

We keep only a take where the song builds and breathes; expect 5 or more tries per prompt.

  1. Write the style prompt in plain descriptive words, in this order:
    • Genre first.
    • Specific instruments (e.g. "fingerpicked acoustic guitar, soft tanpura drone, warm piano").
    • Mood and energy.
    • BPM.
    • What to avoid, written as "no [element]" (e.g. "no heavy drums").
  2. Keep it to about 10 ideas or fewer. Too many signals flatten the result.
  3. Generate the instrumental only: add [Instrumental] so no AI voice is left in the track.
  4. Generate a separate version with AI vocals on our lyrics. This is only a melody guide for the singer; it never goes out.
  5. Judge each take on arrangement, not just sound:
    • Do verse and chorus feel different?
    • Is there space, quiet and build?
    • Fewer instruments usually suit inward songs.
  6. If three takes miss completely, rewrite the prompt rather than retrying it.
  7. Download the chosen instrumental as WAV, and the guide vocal version.

Stage 3: Record vocals

A dry, close vocal in a soft room matters more than any plugin.

Room

  • Use the softest room: bed, curtains, full cupboard. Hang blankets behind and around the singer.
  • Switch off fans and AC. Keep away from windows.

Mic

  • Singer 15–20 cm from the mic, the same distance on every take.
  • Use a pop filter (a sock or thin cloth over a wire hanger works).
  • Set the level so the loudest part peaks around −10 to −6 dB in Cakewalk, never in the red.

Session

  1. Singer learns the melody from the AI guide vocal, then adds their own phrasing and breath.
  2. Singer wears earphones or IEMs so the instrumental doesn't leak into the mic.
  3. Record dry, with no effects.
  4. Record 5–10 takes of each section.
  5. Comp: stitch together the best-sung version of each line.
  6. Re-record any line that is clearly off. A good take beats a corrected one.

Stage 4: Mix in Cakewalk

The goal is one record, not a voice pasted on a track. Restraint first: amateurs overuse reverb, EQ and compression.

  1. Import the Suno instrumental and the comped vocal.
  2. Listen to the instrumental quietly, loudly and in headphones for leftover AI vocal traces.
  3. Vocal chain, in this order:
    1. EQ (TDR Nova): cut low rumble below about 80–100 Hz.
    2. Pitch correction (Graillon): only on phrases that need it, moved to a separate track. Low amount, slow speed, check on and off.
    3. Compression: 2–4 dB of reduction, slow attack.
    4. De-esser: soften harsh "s" sounds.
  4. One shared reverb (Valhalla Supermassive) on both vocal and instrumental, kept subtle.
  5. Lower the instrumental slightly wherever the voice sings, so the voice sits on top.
  6. Compare often with the reference song.
  7. Check on a phone speaker and cheap earbuds, not only IEMs.
  8. Listen again the next day before calling it done.

Stage 5: Master

Master lightly to about −14 LUFS, the loudness YouTube and Spotify play at.

  1. Export the final mix from Cakewalk as WAV, with peaks below 0 dB.
  2. Upload it to BandLab's free mastering and pick the gentlest preset that sounds good.
  3. Compare the master with the reference song at the same volume.
  4. Check on phone speaker, earbuds and IEMs once more.
  5. Keep all masters at similar loudness so the channel sounds consistent.

For our most important releases, consider paying an engineer to mix and master (roughly ₹3,000–10,000 per song).

Stage 6: Animated video

Style is free to change song to song; it stays consistent within one video. A few threads repeat across every video so people recognise us.

Recurring threads (decide once, keep forever)

  • One small recurring symbol in every video.
  • The same title card or logo at the start or end.
  • A signature moment, e.g. each video ends on a character turning inward.

Steps

  1. Storyboard to the song: what happens in intro, each verse, chorus, bridge, outro.
  2. Pick the visual style for this song.
  3. Make a character sheet for each main character: front, side and 3/4 views. Generate 4 versions and pick the best.
  4. Make one keyframe image per shot, using the character sheet as reference.
  5. Animate each keyframe with image-to-video, not text-to-video.
  6. For long shots, save the last frame of a clip and use it to start the next.
  7. Prefer few, slow, poetic shots over busy action.
  8. Video editor cuts the clips to the music and adds the title card.

Test free first on one 30-second section. Pay for one tool only when releasing, to remove watermarks.

Stage 7: Release and short clips

Short-form video is how new listeners find songs; a song people save and finish matters more than raw clicks.

  1. Run the three questions from the Foundation page:
    • Is it inward and inclusive?
    • Does it show rather than tell, and stay free of judgment?
    • Are we giving with this song, or wanting something back?
  2. Video editor makes 10–20 short vertical clips per song. Rotate formats:
    • The hook.
    • A single lyric line.
    • The recording session.
    • Making-of the animation.
  3. Post the same clips on YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels and other short-form platforms.
  4. Release on a steady rhythm, every 2–4 weeks, and keep posting clips between releases.
  5. Be open that the music is AI-assisted, with our own lyrics and real vocals.
  6. When a song flops or takes off, come back to the Foundation page before deciding what's next.
